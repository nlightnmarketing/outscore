Acids, Bases and Salts: Introduction to Indicators

Welcome! In this activity, we'll explore indicators - special substances that help us identify acids and bases without tasting them. Let's discover the colorful world of chemical indicators!

What are Indicators?

You already know that acids taste sour and bases taste bitter. However, we should NEVER taste chemicals as they can be dangerous! Instead, we use INDICATORS - special substances that change colour in the presence of acids or bases.

⚠️ Safety First!

Never taste chemicals to identify them. Always use indicators or other safe testing methods. Many acids and bases are corrosive and can cause serious harm if ingested.

Litmus: The Most Common Indicator

The most common and widely used indicator is LITMUS. It's a purple dye extracted from lichen (a type of plant). When neutral, litmus solution is purple.

How Litmus Works

  • Blue litmus turns RED in acids
  • Red litmus turns BLUE in bases

Litmus is available as both solution and paper strips, making it easy to test substances.

Synthetic Indicators

Besides litmus, there are other common indicators made in laboratories. These are called synthetic indicators:

1. Phenolphthalein

  • Colourless in acids
  • Pink/Magenta in bases

Phenolphthalein is one of the most commonly used indicators in laboratory experiments.

2. Methyl Orange

  • Red in acids
  • Yellow in bases

Methyl orange provides a clear colour distinction between acidic and basic solutions.

Natural Indicators

Nature provides us with many indicators found in plants! These are called natural indicators and can be found in everyday items:

1. Turmeric

  • Yellow normally
  • Turns reddish-brown in bases

Real-life example: That's why curry stains on cloth turn red when you apply soap! Soap is basic, so it changes turmeric's colour from yellow to reddish-brown.

2. Red Cabbage

  • Red in acids
  • Green in bases

Red cabbage juice is an excellent natural indicator that shows clear colour changes.

3. Flower Petals

The coloured petals of certain flowers also act as natural indicators:

  • Hydrangea - Changes colour based on soil pH
  • Petunia - Shows different colours in acids and bases
  • Geranium - Another flower that can indicate pH

Olfactory Indicators

Some indicators don't change colour - instead, they change their odour! These are called olfactory indicators (olfactory means related to smell).

1. Onion

  • Strong smell in acids and neutral solutions
  • No smell in bases

The characteristic smell of onion disappears when it comes in contact with bases.

2. Vanilla

  • Sweet smell normally and in acids
  • No smell in bases

Vanilla essence loses its pleasant smell when added to basic solutions.

3. Clove Oil

Clove oil changes its odour when it comes in contact with acids or bases, making it useful as an olfactory indicator.

Practical Examples

Example 1: Testing Lemon Juice

Question: What happens when blue litmus paper is dipped in lemon juice?

Answer: It turns red!

Explanation: Lemon juice contains citric acid, which is acidic in nature. Acids turn blue litmus paper red. This is a quick and reliable test for identifying acids.

Example 2: Turmeric and Soap

Observation: Turmeric stain on a white cloth turns reddish-brown when soap is applied.

What does this indicate? Soap is basic in nature.

Explanation: Turmeric is a natural indicator that turns reddish-brown in the presence of bases. Since soap turns turmeric reddish-brown, soap is basic in nature. That's why curry stains change colour when you wash with soap!

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1: Confusing Indicator Colours

Wrong: Thinking litmus turns green or white

Correct: Litmus only shows red (in acids) or blue (in bases). It doesn't turn green or white.

Mistake 2: Not Recognizing Natural Indicators

Wrong: Thinking only laboratory chemicals can be indicators

Correct: Many natural substances like turmeric, red cabbage, and flower petals are excellent indicators!

Mistake 3: Confusing Turmeric's Colour Change

Wrong: Thinking turmeric turns reddish-brown in acids

Correct: Turmeric turns reddish-brown in bases, not acids. This is why soap (a base) changes curry stains.

Key Points to Remember

  • Indicators change colour to identify acids and bases
  • Blue litmus → Red in acids; Red litmus → Blue in bases
  • Phenolphthalein: Colourless in acids, Pink in bases
  • Methyl orange: Red in acids, Yellow in bases
  • Natural indicators: Turmeric, red cabbage, flower petals (Hydrangea, Petunia, Geranium)
  • Olfactory indicators: Onion (strong smell in acids/neutral, no smell in bases), Vanilla (sweet smell normally, no smell in bases), Clove oil (changes odour)
  • Safety: Never taste chemicals - always use indicators!

Summary

Excellent! You now know how to identify acids and bases using various types of indicators:

  • Litmus - The most common indicator (blue→red in acids, red→blue in bases)
  • Synthetic indicators - Phenolphthalein and Methyl orange made in laboratories
  • Natural indicators - Found in plants like turmeric, red cabbage, and flower petals
  • Olfactory indicators - Change odour instead of colour (onion, vanilla, clove oil)

Understanding indicators is the first step in mastering acids, bases, and salts. Practice identifying different types of indicators and their color changes to strengthen your knowledge.

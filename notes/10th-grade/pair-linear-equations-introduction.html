Pair of Linear Equations: Introduction and Forming Equations

Welcome! In this chapter, we'll learn how to solve problems involving two unknown quantities by forming and solving a pair of linear equations in two variables!

Introduction

Many real-world problems involve finding two unknown quantities. To solve such problems, we need to form and solve a pair of linear equations in two variables.

We need two equations because we have two unknowns. Each equation provides a relationship between the two variables.

Example: Forming Equations from Word Problems

Let's understand how to form equations with a real-world example:

Problem: Akhila at the Fair

Akhila went to a fair. She had x rides on the Giant Wheel (₹3 each) and played Hoopla y times (₹4 each). The number of Hoopla games is half the number of rides. She spent ₹20 total.

What information do we have?

  • Cost per Giant Wheel ride: ₹3
  • Cost per Hoopla game: ₹4
  • Number of Hoopla games = half the number of rides
  • Total amount spent: ₹20

Forming the Equations

We can form two equations from this problem:

Equation 1: Relation between rides and games

"The number of Hoopla games is half the number of rides"

y = x/2

This can also be written as: 2y = x or x - 2y = 0

Equation 2: Total cost

"She spent ₹20 total"

Cost of x rides at ₹3 each = 3x
Cost of y Hoopla games at ₹4 each = 4y
Total cost = 3x + 4y = 20

3x + 4y = 20

Together, these two equations form a pair of linear equations in two variables:

  • y = x/2 (or x - 2y = 0)
  • 3x + 4y = 20

General Form of a Pair of Linear Equations

A pair of linear equations in two variables has the general form:

a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0

a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0

Where:

  • a₁, a₂, b₁, b₂, c₁, c₂ are real numbers
  • a₁² + b₁² ≠ 0 (at least one of a₁ or b₁ is not zero)
  • a₂² + b₂² ≠ 0 (at least one of a₂ or b₂ is not zero)

The condition a₁² + b₂² ≠ 0 ensures that the equation actually involves both variables (or at least one), making it a meaningful linear equation.

What is a Solution?

A SOLUTION of a pair of linear equations is an ordered pair (x, y) that satisfies BOTH equations simultaneously.

For a pair (x, y) to be a solution, it must make both equations true when we substitute the values of x and y.

Example: Verifying a Solution

Let's check if x = 4 and y = 2 is a solution to our pair of equations from Akhila's problem:

Equation 1: y = x/2

Substituting x = 4, y = 2: 2 = 4/2 = 2 ✓

Equation 2: 3x + 4y = 20

Substituting x = 4, y = 2: 3(4) + 4(2) = 12 + 8 = 20 ✓

Since (4, 2) satisfies both equations, it is a solution to the pair of equations!

Understanding Through Examples

Example 1: Forming Equations from Word Problems

Problem: The sum of two numbers is 15 and their difference is 3. If the two numbers are x and y, which pair of equations represents this situation?

Answer: x + y = 15 and x - y = 3

Explanation:

  • "Sum is 15" means adding the numbers: x + y = 15
  • "Difference is 3" means subtracting: x - y = 3

These two equations form a pair of linear equations. Common mistakes include:

  • Swapping the equations (x - y = 15 and x + y = 3) - wrong!
  • Using product instead of difference (x + y = 15 and xy = 3) - wrong! This creates a non-linear equation
  • Not understanding "sum" and "difference" correctly

Example 2: Verifying a Solution

Problem: Is (2, 3) a solution of the pair of equations x + y = 5 and 2x - y = 1?

Answer: Yes, because it satisfies both equations

Verification:

Equation 1: x + y = 5
Substituting x = 2, y = 3: 2 + 3 = 5 ✓

Equation 2: 2x - y = 1
Substituting x = 2, y = 3: 2(2) - 3 = 4 - 3 = 1 ✓

Since (2, 3) satisfies both equations simultaneously, it is a solution. Remember: A solution must satisfy BOTH equations, not just one!

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1: Confusing Sum and Difference

Wrong: Writing x - y = 15 and x + y = 3 when the problem says "sum is 15 and difference is 3"

Correct: Sum means addition (x + y = 15) and difference means subtraction (x - y = 3). Read word problems carefully!

Mistake 2: Confusing Difference with Product

Wrong: Writing x + y = 15 and xy = 3 when the problem says "difference is 3"

Correct: Difference means subtraction (x - y = 3), while product means multiplication (xy). Using xy creates a non-linear equation!

Mistake 3: Thinking One Equation is Enough

Wrong: Believing a solution only needs to satisfy one equation

Correct: A solution must satisfy BOTH equations simultaneously. That's why we need a pair of equations!

Mistake 4: Calculation Errors in Verification

Wrong: Making arithmetic mistakes when substituting values

Correct: Always check your calculations carefully. For example, 2 + 3 = 5, and 2(2) - 3 = 4 - 3 = 1.

Key Points to Remember

  • Real-world problems can often be represented as a pair of linear equations
  • We need two equations because we have two unknowns
  • A solution must satisfy BOTH equations simultaneously
  • The general form of a pair of linear equations is:
    • a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0
    • a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0
  • There are several methods to solve these: graphical and algebraic methods
  • When forming equations from word problems:
    • Sum means addition (x + y)
    • Difference means subtraction (x - y)
    • Product means multiplication (xy)

Summary

Great start! You now understand how to form a pair of linear equations from word problems. Here's what we've learned:

  • Many real-world problems involve two unknown quantities, requiring a pair of linear equations
  • We need two equations because we have two unknowns - each equation provides a relationship
  • The general form is: a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0 and a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0
  • A solution is an ordered pair (x, y) that satisfies both equations simultaneously
  • When forming equations from word problems, carefully identify:
    • What the variables represent
    • What relationships are given (sum, difference, product, etc.)
    • How to translate words into mathematical equations
  • Common mistakes include confusing sum/difference/product, or thinking one equation is enough

Understanding how to form equations from word problems is the first step. Practice identifying relationships and translating them into mathematical equations to strengthen your skills.

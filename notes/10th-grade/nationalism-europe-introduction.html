Nationalism in Europe: Introduction to Nationalism

Welcome! In this chapter, we will explore one of the most powerful forces that shaped modern Europe – NATIONALISM. During the nineteenth century, nationalism transformed the political landscape of Europe, leading to the creation of nation-states as we know them today.

What is Nationalism?

Nationalism is a sense of collective identity and belonging that people feel towards their nation. It is the belief that people who share a common language, history, culture, or territory belong together as a nation and should govern themselves.

In the 19th century, this idea was revolutionary because most of Europe was ruled by powerful monarchs and large multi-national empires, not by the people themselves.

Nationalism challenged the existing order where:

  • Powerful monarchs ruled over diverse populations
  • People had no say in their governance
  • Different ethnic and cultural groups were forced to live together under one ruler
  • There was no sense of shared identity or common purpose

What is a Nation-State?

A nation-state is a country where:

  • The majority of citizens share a common identity
  • They have a sense of shared history and culture
  • A centralised government exercises sovereign control over a defined territory
  • The people (not just rulers) feel connected to the nation

Before the 19th Century

Before the 19th century, most of Europe was divided into:

  • Kingdoms - ruled by kings
  • Duchies - ruled by dukes
  • Empires - large territories with diverse populations

In these systems, different peoples lived together under one ruler, often without any common identity. The people were subjects, not citizens with a sense of belonging to a nation.

Key Difference

Old System: People were subjects ruled by a monarch
Nation-State: People are citizens who share a common identity and participate in shaping their nation's destiny

The Vision of a New World

In 1848, French artist Frédéric Sorrieu created a series of prints imagining a world of 'democratic and social Republics'. This was a powerful visual representation of the nationalist vision.

What Sorrieu's Vision Showed

In his vision:

  • People from Europe and America march together in a long procession
  • They offer homage to a statue of Liberty holding a torch and the Charter of Rights of Man
  • Different nations are shown as distinct groups, identified by their flags and national costumes
  • The symbols of absolutist power (unlimited monarchical power) lie shattered on the ground

This was a UTOPIAN vision – an ideal society that seemed unlikely to exist but inspired people to fight for change.

Sorrieu's vision represented the hope that:

  • People would govern themselves through democratic republics
  • Different nations would coexist peacefully
  • The old system of absolute monarchy would be destroyed
  • Liberty and rights would be protected for all

Key Concepts to Remember

Understanding these terms is essential for studying nationalism in Europe:

Absolutist

A government with no limits on its power, usually a centralised, militarised monarchy. The ruler has absolute (unlimited) authority and citizens have no say in governance.

Utopian

A vision of an ideal society that is so perfect it seems unlikely to actually exist. It represents hopes and dreams rather than reality, but can inspire people to work for change.

Sovereignty

Supreme power or authority over a territory. In a nation-state, sovereignty means the government has complete control over its territory and people, without interference from outside powers.

Nation

A large group of people who share common characteristics like language, history, culture, or territory and see themselves as belonging together. They feel a sense of collective identity and often desire self-governance.

Understanding Through Examples

Example 1: What is a Nation-State?

Question: What is a nation-state?

Answer: A country where the majority of citizens share a common identity and sense of shared history

Explanation: A nation-state is one where the majority of citizens develop a sense of common identity and shared history or descent, and a centralised government exercises sovereign control over a defined territory. This is different from:

  • Traditional kingdoms/empires - where people were just subjects ruled by a monarch
  • Military alliances - where different countries form partnerships
  • Colonies - where foreign powers rule over the people

In a nation-state, the people themselves feel connected to their nation and participate in shaping its destiny.

Example 2: Understanding Absolutism

Question: The term 'absolutist' in the context of 19th century Europe refers to:

Answer: A government or system of rule that has no restraints on the power exercised

Explanation: Absolutist refers to a form of monarchical government that was centralised, militarised, and repressive, with no limits on the ruler's power. This is the opposite of:

  • Democratic governments - where people elect representatives
  • Governments with freedom - absolutist governments gave power to rulers, not freedom to citizens
  • Federal systems - where power is shared, not concentrated in one ruler

The term 'absolute' here means the ruler has unlimited power, not that citizens have absolute freedom.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1: Confusing Nation-State with Traditional Monarchies

Wrong: Thinking a nation-state is just any territory ruled by a powerful king

Correct: A nation-state is different because the PEOPLE share a common identity and feel connected to their nation, not just ruled by a monarch. The key is the sense of shared identity among citizens.

Mistake 2: Confusing Absolutism with Democracy

Wrong: Thinking absolutist means democratic or giving freedom to citizens

Correct: Absolutist governments had NO limits on the ruler's power and were NOT elected. 'Absolute' refers to the ruler's unlimited power, not citizens' freedom.

Mistake 3: Confusing Nation-State with Alliances or Colonies

Wrong: Thinking a nation-state is a military alliance or a colony

Correct: A nation-state is a single country where people share a common identity and govern themselves. Alliances involve multiple countries, and colonies lack self-governance.

Key Points to Remember

  • Nationalism emerged as a powerful force in 19th century Europe
  • It led to the creation of nation-states in place of multi-national empires
  • A nation-state is where citizens share a common identity and history
  • This sense of commonness was FORGED through struggles and actions of leaders and common people
  • The idea challenged the absolute power of monarchs and empires
  • Key Concepts:
    • Absolutist: Government with no limits on power (centralised, militarised monarchy)
    • Utopian: Vision of an ideal society that seems unlikely but inspires change
    • Sovereignty: Supreme power or authority over a territory
    • Nation: Large group sharing common characteristics (language, history, culture, territory)

Summary

Great start! You now understand the basic concepts of nationalism and nation-states. Here's what we've learned:

  • Nationalism is a sense of collective identity and belonging that people feel towards their nation
  • In the 19th century, nationalism was revolutionary - it challenged monarchs and empires
  • A nation-state is where citizens share a common identity, history, and are governed by a centralised authority
  • Before the 19th century, Europe was divided into kingdoms, duchies, and empires without shared identity
  • Frédéric Sorrieu's 1848 vision showed a utopian world of democratic republics with liberty and rights
  • Key concepts include:
    • Absolutist - unlimited monarchical power
    • Utopian - ideal but unlikely vision
    • Sovereignty - supreme authority over territory
    • Nation - people sharing common characteristics
  • Nationalism transformed Europe by creating nation-states where people governed themselves

Understanding nationalism helps explain how modern Europe was shaped. The idea that people with shared identity should govern themselves was revolutionary and led to major political transformations throughout the 19th century.

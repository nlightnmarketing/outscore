Nationalism in India: Introduction to the Anti-Colonial Movement

Welcome! In the previous chapter, you learned how nationalism emerged in Europe. Now we explore how NATIONALISM developed in INDIA. Here, modern nationalism is intimately connected to the ANTI-COLONIAL MOVEMENT against British rule.

Nationalism and Colonialism

In India, unlike Europe, the growth of nationalism was linked to the struggle against COLONIAL RULE. This is a key difference from European nationalism.

People discovered their UNITY in the process of their struggle with colonialism. The sense of being OPPRESSED under colonialism provided a shared bond.

Key Characteristics of Indian Nationalism

  • This bond tied MANY DIFFERENT GROUPS together - peasants, workers, tribals, women, and various classes
  • Different classes and groups felt the effects of colonialism DIFFERENTLY
  • Their experiences were varied and their notions of freedom were NOT ALWAYS THE SAME
  • The Congress under Mahatma Gandhi tried to forge these diverse groups together within ONE MOVEMENT

Important Understanding

Indian nationalism was not uniform. Different groups had different reasons for opposing colonialism and different visions of what freedom meant. The challenge was to unite them in a common struggle.

The First World War and Its Effects

The years after 1919 saw the national movement spreading to new areas and developing new modes of struggle. The First World War (1914-1918) created new economic and political conditions that fueled discontent.

Economic Hardships Caused by the War

1. Increased Defence Expenditure

Huge increase in DEFENCE EXPENDITURE financed by war loans and increased taxes. This burden fell heavily on the Indian people.

2. Price Rise

PRICES DOUBLED between 1913 and 1918 – causing extreme hardship for common people. The cost of living increased dramatically while wages did not keep pace.

3. Forced Recruitment

Villages were forced to supply SOLDIERS – forced recruitment caused widespread anger in rural areas. People were taken away from their families and livelihoods against their will.

4. Crop Failures

CROP FAILURES in 1918-19 and 1920-21 led to acute food shortages. Agriculture was disrupted, and many farmers lost their livelihoods.

5. Influenza Epidemic

INFLUENZA EPIDEMIC – according to the 1921 census, 12-13 MILLION people perished. This was one of the deadliest pandemics in history, and it hit India particularly hard.

People hoped hardships would end after the war. But that did NOT happen. The British continued their policies, and discontent grew.

A New Leader, A New Method

At this stage of discontent, a NEW LEADER appeared with a NEW MODE OF STRUGGLE.

Mahatma Gandhi's Return

Mahatma Gandhi returned to India in January 1915. He had come from SOUTH AFRICA where he had successfully fought the racist regime with a novel method of mass agitation called SATYAGRAHA.

Satyagraha means "truth-force" or "soul-force" - a method of non-violent resistance that would transform India's freedom struggle.

Early Satyagraha Movements in India

After arriving in India, Gandhi organised satyagraha movements in various places:

1917: Champaran (Bihar)

Satyagraha against the oppressive plantation system. Indigo farmers were forced to grow indigo on a portion of their land at low prices. Gandhi's intervention led to the Champaran Agrarian Act, which improved conditions for farmers.

1917: Kheda (Gujarat)

Peasants were affected by crop failure and plague. Despite poor harvests, the British insisted on collecting full revenue. Gandhi led a satyagraha demanding suspension of revenue collection, which was eventually granted.

1918: Ahmedabad

Gandhi organised cotton mill workers in a strike for better wages. This showed that satyagraha could be used for industrial disputes as well as agrarian issues.

These early successes showed the power of his new method. Satyagraha proved to be an effective tool for mass mobilization and non-violent resistance.

India's National Movement – Key Phases

The national movement developed through several major phases, each building on the previous one:

1. Non-Cooperation Movement (1920-22)

  • Combined with the Khilafat Movement - uniting Hindus and Muslims
  • People were asked to boycott British institutions, schools, courts, and foreign goods
  • Withdrawn after Chauri Chaura violence - Gandhi believed in strict non-violence
  • Despite being withdrawn, it showed the power of mass mobilization

2. Civil Disobedience Movement (1930-34)

  • Began with the famous Salt March (Dandi March) in 1930
  • People broke salt laws and other British regulations
  • Gandhi-Irwin Pact (1931) - temporary truce
  • Round Table Conference - discussions about India's future
  • Movement suspended and resumed, showing the persistence of the struggle

3. Quit India Movement (1942)

  • 'Do or Die' call by Gandhi
  • Final push for complete independence
  • Massive participation despite severe British repression
  • Paved the way for India's independence in 1947

Focus of This Chapter

In this chapter, we will focus on the Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience Movements - two of the most significant phases of India's freedom struggle.

Understanding Through Examples

Example 1: Difference Between Indian and European Nationalism

Question: How is the growth of modern nationalism in India different from nationalism in Europe?

Answer: In India, nationalism is intimately connected to the anti-colonial movement against British rule

Explanation: Unlike Europe where nationalism emerged through cultural and political movements, in India modern nationalism developed through the shared struggle against British colonial rule. People discovered their unity through their experience of colonial oppression. This is fundamentally different from European nationalism, which was about creating nation-states from existing kingdoms.

Example 2: Consequences of World War I

Question: Which of the following was NOT a consequence of the First World War that fueled Indian discontent?

Answer: "Complete withdrawal of British administration from India"

Explanation: The British did NOT withdraw their administration. In fact, they increased taxes and repression. The other options were all real consequences:

  • Prices doubled between 1913 and 1918 - causing extreme hardship
  • Forced recruitment of soldiers - causing widespread anger in villages
  • Crop failures - leading to food shortages and famine

The war created economic hardship, but the British administration remained and even became more repressive.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1: Thinking Indian Nationalism Was Independent

Wrong: Believing nationalism in India developed independent of any external struggle

Correct: Indian nationalism was intimately CONNECTED to the anti-colonial movement. People discovered their unity through their struggle against British colonialism. The shared experience of oppression created the bond of nationalism.

Mistake 2: Limiting Nationalism to Elite

Wrong: Thinking nationalism was led only by the educated elite

Correct: While educated leaders played a role, the national movement involved MANY different groups – peasants, workers, tribals, and women from various classes. Different groups had different experiences and notions of freedom.

Mistake 3: Confusing War Consequences

Wrong: Thinking the British withdrew after World War I or that all hardships ended

Correct: The British did NOT withdraw. Prices doubled, forced recruitment continued, crop failures occurred, and millions died from famine and influenza. People hoped hardships would end, but they did not.
